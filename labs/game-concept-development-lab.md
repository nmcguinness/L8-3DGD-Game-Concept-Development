# Overview

This lab puts the tools from **Game Concept Development — Note** to work on your own concept. You work in pairs as critical friends: each of you develops your own concept, and your partner's job is to push it, not to agree with it.

| Verb | Think of it as | What it changes |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Substitute | Swapping the theme to see what breaks | Whether the theme reaches the rules |
| Stretch | Writing safe, stretched and radical versions | How far the concept reaches |
| Pressure | Asking what the mechanic forces the character to do | How deep the character goes |
| Report | Recording what the process revealed | What you carry into the questionnaire |

The paired note is delivered in the 15 minutes before this lab, so the whole session runs to one hour.

| Time | Activity | Checkpoint |
| :- | :- | :- |
| 0:00–0:02 | Setup | |
| 0:02–0:12 | Exercises 01–03: Theme drives mechanic | Checkpoint 1 |
| 0:12–0:22 | Exercises 04–05: Stretch the concept | Checkpoint 2 |
| 0:22–0:35 | Exercises 06–08: Build the character | Checkpoint 3 |
| 0:35–0:45 | Exercise 09: Findings report | Submitted |

This lab directly feeds your individual concept questionnaire on Moodle, which closes at 11am on Monday 28 September. The supervision panel reviews submissions in Week 3.

# Learning outcomes

By the end of this lab you can:

1. Write a core-verb sentence for your concept that passes the substitution test.
2. Identify what your rules argue about your theme, and any risk of ludonarrative dissonance.
3. Choose between three concept variants using a named MDA aesthetic, and rate the risk.
4. Build a four-quadrant character profile with a playable consequence in each quadrant.
5. Write a character spine and name how the backstory will be delivered through play.
6. Report your pair's findings in a form the class can learn from.

---

# Ground rules

- **Your concept stays yours.** Each of you submits your own concept individually. Do not merge concepts with your partner.
- **Anonymity.** Questionnaire submissions stay anonymous to the panel. Keep your name off your concept answers, and do not describe your partner's concept to other pairs.
- **One worksheet each.** All outputs go into your own `concept_worksheet.md`. The findings report is the only shared output.
- **A presents first, B probes, then swap.** Keep to time; your partner's turn matters as much as yours.
- **Checkpoints are gates.** Do not start the next group until the lecturer has signed off the checkpoint. If a checkpoint is not passed, fix the named gap in the next two minutes, then continue.

# Theme drives mechanic

## Exercise 01 — Write the core verb

Both partners at once (2 minutes). Using the template from Note Section 1, write your concept's core verb.

> The player [verb] [object] in order to [goal], and this expresses the theme because [reason].

**Output:** `core_verb`

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Borrowed Time: "The player spends seconds from a borrowed lifespan to use abilities, in order to reach their daughter, and this expresses the theme because every action is paid for with time owed to someone else."

Thin Places: "The player looks through and moves between two overlapping layers of the same space, in order to solve problems neither world can solve alone, and this expresses the theme because the thin boundary is the only place the two worlds meet."

</details>

> Write 2–3 sentences:
> - What verb did you choose?
> - Is it a verb the player repeats, or a one-off event?

## Exercise 02 — Run the substitution test

A presents (2 minutes), then swap (2 minutes). The partner who is listening names a different theme and asks: "What breaks?" Classify the result using the table in Note Section 1.2.

**Output:** `substitution_result` (Nothing changes / Only story and art change / Core rules stop working, plus one line on why)

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Weak concept: "A platformer where a timer counts down." Substitute *ghosts* and the countdown still works. Result: Nothing changes.

Strong concept (Borrowed Time, above): substitute *ghosts* and there is nothing to spend, no debt and no collectors. Result: Core rules stop working.

</details>

> Write 2–3 sentences:
> - Which result did your concept get?
> - What does that result tell you about where your theme currently lives: the rules or the fiction?

## Exercise 03 — What do your rules argue?

A presents (2 minutes), then swap (2 minutes). The listener asks two questions:

1. "Stripped of art, text and sound, what do your rules say about the theme?"
2. "Does play ever reward the opposite of what your theme claims?"

Rewrite your core verb if needed.

**Output:** `rules_argument` (one line on what the rules argue, one line on any dissonance risk)

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Rules argue: "Time is finite and borrowed; spending it on others repays it, hoarding it has a cost."

Dissonance risk: "If time pickups are scattered freely around the level, the player never feels the debt. Pickups should come only from repaying others."

</details>

> Write 2–3 sentences:
> - What changed in your core verb after this exercise, if anything?
> - What single rule would you add or remove to make the argument clearer?

**Checkpoint 1.** Show the lecturer both partners' `core_verb` and `substitution_result`. Pass when each core verb names a repeated action, and the substitution test breaks at least one rule of play.

# Stretch the concept

## Exercise 04 — Write three variants

Both partners at once (3 minutes). Write safe, stretched and radical versions of your concept, one line each. Use the questions in Note Section 2.2.

**Output:** `variants`

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Thin Places:

- Safe: third-person horror in a forest where ghosts appear near thin spots.
- Stretched: two local co-op players, each able to see only one layer of the world, who must talk each other through it.
- Radical: VR, where leaning your head through a thin spot shows the other layer, and objects moved in one world shift in the other.

</details>

> Write 2–3 sentences:
> - Which variant was hardest to write, and why?
> - Which one would you be most proud to demo at the end of the year?

## Exercise 05 — Choose and justify

A presents (3 minutes), then swap (3 minutes). The listener picks the variant they find most interesting and argues for it. The presenter then decides, stating:

- the target aesthetic (MDA), in one or two words
- the amplifier used, if any
- the risk rating (Low / Medium / High), with a reason if High

**Output:** `chosen_variant`

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

"Radical variant. Target aesthetic: disorientation and discovery. Amplifier: VR on Quest 3, because leaning and looking make the thin boundary physical. Risk: Medium; headsets are available, and the main risk is holding frame rate while rendering two layers, which we would prototype first."

</details>

> Write 2–3 sentences:
> - Did your partner's choice match yours? What did they see that you did not?
> - What would you need to prototype first to prove the chosen variant is buildable?

**Checkpoint 2.** Show the lecturer each partner's `chosen_variant`. Pass when the choice is justified by the theme and the target aesthetic, not just by novelty, and any High risk has a stated reason.

# Build the character

## Exercise 06 — Fill the four quadrants

Both partners at once (5 minutes). For each quadrant in Note Section 3.2, write one detail and its playable consequence.

**Output:** `quadrant_table`

| Quadrant | Detail | Playable consequence |
| :- | :- | :- |
| 1. Physical | | |
| 2. Psychological | | |
| 3. Interpersonal | | |
| 4. History | | |

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

| Quadrant | Detail | Playable consequence |
| :- | :- | :- |
| 1. Physical | Cal has one damaged eye | The second layer appears only on one side of view in VR |
| 2. Psychological | He believes he can always tell the worlds apart | Late in the game, layers bleed together and the player cannot either |
| 3. Interpersonal | His sister vanished through a thin place | Her traces appear only in the other layer |
| 4. History | He mapped the boundary before, and lost her doing it | Old map fragments are embedded in both layers |

</details>

> Write 2–3 sentences:
> - Which quadrant had no playable consequence at first?
> - Which detail are you now most likely to cut, and why?

## Exercise 07 — Apply pressure

A presents (3 minutes), then swap (3 minutes). The listener asks:

1. "What does your mechanic force this character to do that they would rather not?"
2. "What can't they do?"
3. "Does the mechanic ever make them act against who you say they are?"

**Output:** `pressure_notes` (one line per question)

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

1. Ada must spend time she owes on strangers to keep the debt manageable, when all she wants is to save it for her daughter.
2. She cannot rewind; spent time is gone.
3. Risk: if combat is the fastest way to earn time, Ada becomes a killer for time, which contradicts a mother trying to repay a debt. Earning time should come from helping, not fighting.

</details>

> Write 2–3 sentences:
> - Which question was hardest to answer?
> - What is one design change the pressure questions have pushed you towards?

## Exercise 08 — Write the spine and choose a delivery mode

Both partners at once (2 minutes). Write your character spine, then name which of Jenkins' four modes (Note Section 4.2) will deliver the History quadrant.

> [Character] wants [X] but is held back by [Y]; the player feels this through [mechanic Z].

**Output:** `spine`

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

"Ada wants one more year with her daughter but owes it to someone she has never met; the player feels this every time a jump or a rescue costs seconds. Delivery: embedded (fragments of the bargain found in the world) and enacted (repaying the debt through play)."

</details>

> Write 2–3 sentences:
> - Does your spine name a mechanic, or only a story beat?
> - Why is your chosen delivery mode better than a cutscene for this character?

**Checkpoint 3.** Show the lecturer both partners' `spine`. Pass when the spine names a want, an obstacle and a mechanic, the History quadrant links to the theme, and a delivery mode is named.

# Findings report

## Exercise 09 — Pair findings report

Together (7 minutes to write, then the lecturer asks two or three pairs for a 1-minute summary). The report is about what the process revealed, not your concepts, so keep concept detail to a minimum. Submit one report per pair.

**Output:** `findings_report`

1. **Hardest test.** Which tool (substitution, stretch, character pressure) was hardest for your pair, and why?
2. **Before and after.** For each partner, one line on what changed in the concept during the lab.
3. **Theme link.** For each concept, rate how strongly the theme now drives the mechanic (surface / partial / core), with one reason.
4. **Risk.** The biggest risk in either concept, including any risk of ludonarrative dissonance, and how you would reduce it.
5. **Open question.** One question you still need answered before submitting.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

1. Hardest test: character pressure. We both had characters with abilities but no limits, and "what can't they do?" exposed that.
2. Before and after: A moved from a countdown timer to spending borrowed time on abilities. B moved from haunted-forest horror to a VR two-layer puzzle game.
3. Theme link: A core, because removing the theme removes the resource economy. B partial, because the layers are core, but the horror elements are still surface.
4. Risk: B's VR frame rate with two layers rendered. Reduce by prototyping the layer switch in a greybox scene first.
5. Open question: can one level in the second layer be streamed in rather than rendered at the same time?

</details>

> Write 2–3 sentences:
> - What pattern did you notice across both concepts?
> - What is the first thing each of you will change in your questionnaire answers tonight?

# Common mistakes

| Mistake | Symptom | Fix |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Countdown timer as the time mechanic | Substitution test: nothing changes | Make time a resource the player spends, owes or trades |
| Setting mistaken for theme | "It's in a haunted place" is the whole theme link | Define the rule that only exists because of the thin place |
| Amplifier bolted on | VR or multiplayer added with no link to the aesthetic | Remove it, or show how it intensifies the target feeling |
| Character as dossier | Quadrants full of appearance and preferences | Cut any detail with no playable consequence |
| Abilities instead of limits | Pressure question 2 has no answer | State one thing the character cannot do, and make the player feel it |
| Spine without a mechanic | Spine ends in a story beat, not a player action | Rewrite the "felt through" clause as something the player does |
| Partner agrees with everything | No changes recorded in the findings report | Listener must argue for a variant the presenter did not pick |
| Concepts merged | Two worksheets describe the same game | Separate them; the questionnaire is an individual submission |

# Theme sources

Games are not the only place to find a theme worked out in depth. Writers, artists, composers and film-makers have treated both of this year's themes for centuries, and they often find rules and costs that game designers miss.

Use these outside the lab. Pick one or two works for your theme; you do not need to cover the list. When you examine a work, ask the same questions you asked of your own concept:

> What rule does this world follow? What does it cost? What is the audience made to feel?

## Borrowed Time

Time that is owed, lent, bought, stolen or running out.

| Work | Form | What to look for |
| :- | :- | :- |
| Michael Ende, *Momo* (1973) | Novel | Grey men persuade people to "save" time, which is secretly stolen. Time as an economy with a thief. |
| Oscar Wilde, *The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1890) | Novel | Youth borrowed from a portrait that pays the cost. Where does the debt go when it is hidden? |
| Christopher Marlowe, *Doctor Faustus* (c. 1592) | Play | Twenty-four years bought from the devil. The terms of the bargain, and the final hour. |
| Kazuo Ishiguro, *Never Let Me Go* (2005) | Novel | Lives with a fixed end, lived as if ordinary. How a character behaves when the debt cannot be avoided. |
| Claire North, *The First Fifteen Lives of Harry August* (2014) | Novel | A life relived with knowledge carried over. A close cousin of loop-based games. |
| *Ikiru*, dir. Akira Kurosawa (1952) | Film | A man told he has months to live decides what to spend them on. |
| *The Seventh Seal*, dir. Ingmar Bergman (1957) | Film | A knight plays chess with Death to buy time. A game inside the story, used to delay the end. |
| *In Time*, dir. Andrew Niccol (2011) | Film | Time as literal currency, earned, spent and stolen. Good for economy ideas, weaker on character. |
| *Groundhog Day*, dir. Harold Ramis (1993) | Film | A repeated day as a pressure that changes a character. |
| Hans Holbein the Younger, *The Ambassadors* (1533) | Painting | A distorted skull that only resolves from one angle. Death hidden in plain sight; a perspective mechanic. |
| Dutch vanitas still life, 17th century | Painting | Skulls, candles, hourglasses and rotting fruit as reminders that time is short. A visual language for your art direction. |
| Salvador Dalí, *The Persistence of Memory* (1931) | Painting | Melting clocks: time made soft and unreliable. |
| Christian Marclay, *The Clock* (2010) | Video installation | A 24-hour montage of film clips, synchronised to real time. Real time as the material of the work. |
| Olivier Messiaen, *Quartet for the End of Time* (1941) | Music | Written and first performed in a prisoner-of-war camp. Listen for time that seems to stop. |
| Pink Floyd, "Time" (1973) | Song | Life slipping past unnoticed while waiting for it to begin. |
| Andrew Marvell, "To His Coy Mistress" (1681) | Poem | An argument built entirely on time running out. |
| Philip Larkin, "Aubade" (1977) | Poem | Waking before dawn and facing mortality without comfort. |

## Thin Places

Places where the boundary between two worlds is weak. The term comes from Celtic Christian tradition, and Irish folklore is full of them: fairy forts, holy wells, and Samhain, when the boundary is thinnest.

| Work | Form | What to look for |
| :- | :- | :- |
| China Miéville, *The City & the City* (2009) | Novel | Two cities occupying the same space, whose citizens are trained not to see each other. A near-perfect design brief. |
| Susanna Clarke, *Piranesi* (2020) | Novel | A man living inside an endless house of halls and tides, and what he does not know about how he got there. The player–character gap in novel form. |
| C.S. Lewis, *The Magician's Nephew* (1955) | Novel | The Wood between the Worlds: a quiet in-between place full of pools, each leading to a different world. |
| Lewis Carroll, *Through the Looking-Glass* (1871) | Novel | A mirror as a threshold into a world with inverted rules. |
| Jeff VanderMeer, *Annihilation* (2014) | Novel | An area where the ordinary world is being slowly rewritten. The 2018 film by Alex Garland adapts it freely. |
| *Stalker*, dir. Andrei Tarkovsky (1979) | Film | A guide leads visitors through the Zone, where the rules are unknown and every step is tested. |
| *Spirited Away*, dir. Hayao Miyazaki (2001) | Film | A girl crosses into a spirit world through a tunnel and must work to get back. |
| *Pan's Labyrinth*, dir. Guillermo del Toro (2006) | Film | A fantasy world and a brutal real one, each commenting on the other. |
| *Wings of Desire*, dir. Wim Wenders (1987) | Film | Angels move unseen among the living in Berlin, able to listen but not touch. An observer who cannot act. |
| *Song of the Sea*, dir. Tomm Moore (2014) | Animated film | Made by Cartoon Saloon in Kilkenny. Irish selkie myth, with the spirit world close under the everyday one. |
| Newgrange, Co. Meath (c. 3200 BC) | Monument | A passage tomb built so that sunlight enters the chamber only around the winter solstice. A place where the boundary opens at one time only. |
| René Magritte, *The Human Condition* (1933) | Painting | A painting on an easel that exactly continues the view behind it. Two layers that seem to be one. |
| James Turrell, *Skyspaces* (from the 1970s) | Installation | Rooms with an opening to the sky, where light makes the ceiling edge seem to dissolve. Perception as the boundary. |
| Caspar David Friedrich, *Wanderer above the Sea of Fog* (c. 1818) | Painting | A figure at the edge of the known, facing what cannot be seen clearly. |
| Arvo Pärt, *Spiegel im Spiegel* (1978) | Music | "Mirror in the mirror": a very simple piece that seems to reflect endlessly. |
| Brian Eno, *Ambient 4: On Land* (1982) | Album | Places rendered as sound. A model for audio-led design. |
| "Port na bPúcaí" | Traditional Irish air | From the Blasket Islands, said to have been heard over the sea at night. Music from the other side. |
| W.B. Yeats, "The Stolen Child" (1886) | Poem | Fairies lure a child away from the human world. The pull of the other side, and what is left behind. |
| Seamus Heaney, "Station Island" (1984) | Poem sequence | A pilgrimage to Lough Derg, where the poet meets the dead. A real Irish thin place. |
| T.S. Eliot, "Little Gidding" (1942) | Poem | A place where past and present meet. It bridges both themes. |

Write 2–3 sentences in your worksheet for any work you examine:

> - What rule or cost did the work reveal that your concept does not yet have?
> - Could that rule become a mechanic?
